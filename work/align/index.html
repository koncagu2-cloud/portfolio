Independent product concept · AI-assisted · 2026

ALIGN

Understand the opportunity before committing.

Make a role easier to judge before asking for commitment. I directed a discovery experience that keeps listing facts, personal priorities, and missing information distinct, then brought those rules into search, comparison, and application review.

Try the search results →See what changed ↓

My contributionProduct direction and iterative feedback. AI-assisted design exploration, documentation, implementation and checks.

The project in brief

The problem

A preference match is not a qualification assessment. Candidates need listing facts, their own claims and unanswered questions to stay distinct.

My choice

Pair a short summary of pay and work arrangement with an inspectable Decision Brief. A missing answer stays unknown; a candidate’s statement stays self-declared.

See how the Decision Brief handles uncertainty ↓

What to inspect

Inspect a working candidate journey, the Decision Brief alternatives and the revisions that make unknowns explicit. All listings and candidate data are fictional.

Project context and team
Intended users
Job seekers comparing conditions and evidence before deciding what to pursue.
Scope
Candidate and employer design exploration; a working candidate web prototype.
Contribution

Design decisions

  1. Pair orientation with inspectable evidence ↓

    A concise summary sits beside detailed sources and unknowns, preserving caveats without asking a candidate to start in a long ledger.

  2. Treat owner review as a signal, not user research ↓

    The internal walkthrough led to clearer seniority and confirmation language; independent comprehension and task outcomes remain untested.

  3. Make the demo boundary explicit ↓

    The application flow ends in a local receipt that says employer delivery was not sent.

01 / Product premise

Conditions before commitments.

The product question was how to help a candidate understand a role before committing to an application. The brief focused on three things: conditions that fit their preferences, qualifications they can support and questions the listing leaves unanswered. This framing came from the project brief; it has not been validated through candidate interviews.

Early AI-generated stakeholder scenarios helped generate questions. They were synthetic exploration, not real participants or employer partnerships. A listing fact, a candidate declaration and verified evidence need different meanings in the interface.

02 / Information architecture

From exploring a role to reviewing an application.

  1. Discover

    Explore one role. Save, pass, undo or inspect details using explicit controls.

  2. Search

    Investigate through filters and stable results. Desktop supports list/detail review.

  3. Saved

    Organize private notes and compare the same criteria across a shortlist.

  4. Applications

    Review the application details and see a demo receipt that clearly says nothing was sent to an employer.

Saving is private. It does not apply, express interest or change an employer’s review status. Employer design artifacts remain a separate exploration.

03 / Designing the Decision Brief

Show what fits. Explain what is still unknown.

01 / The problem

A role can meet someone’s pay and location preferences while leaving their qualifications unclear. One positive match summary would blur those different judgments.

02 / My confident choice

I directed the concept toward a short summary with inspectable evidence underneath. Pay and work arrangement provide orientation; self-declared claims, unknowns and explicit gaps retain distinct meanings.

03 / The artifact

Decision Brief · Original design alternative

Self-declared → identify the source
The candidate says they have a UX portfolio. Keep that claim distinct from reviewed work.
Unknown → keep the question open
No answer is available. Do not turn missing information into a lack of experience.
Potential gap → show an explicit answer
“Design systems: not yet” is a stated absence of experience. It means something different from an unanswered field.

04 / The tradeoff

The summary reduces reading effort by moving detail into a second layer. That makes source labels essential: a preference match cannot stand in for a qualification assessment. Independent comprehension testing is the next evaluation priority.

Compare the evidence-ledger alternative

The ledger placed criteria and their sources together in a longer list. It exposed detail, but repeated information and took more space on a phone. The summary-and-evidence direction keeps an initial overview while retaining the detailed distinctions. These are design alternatives; no comparative usability test was conducted.

04 / Owner review and iteration

Make uncertainty and consequences visible.

An internal walkthrough used the project owner’s chat responses and screenshots. Prior product knowledge limits those observations. There were no independent participants or task timings; the mobile walkthrough stopped before completion.

01 / The problem

An owner walkthrough exposed ambiguity in missing seniority, comparison labels and what a demo confirmation meant.

02 / My confident choice

I asked for explicit unknown states, consistent arrangement labels and a receipt that leads with “Employer delivery: Not sent.” Each revision makes the interface’s meaning easier to inspect.

03 / The artifact

Revision evidence · Owner observations and original captures

Missing seniority

Observation: the owner could interpret conditions but could not identify the sought experience level.

Response: state “Not specified in this listing.” Do not infer years from a job title.

Comparison vocabulary

Observation: the owner requested arrangement indicators while comparing roles.

Response: reuse labeled Remote, Hybrid and On-site chips as static facts. No speed improvement was measured.

Confirmation meaning

Observation: the owner’s explanation mixed demo awareness with an expectation of delivery verification.

Response: lead with the local result and “Employer delivery: Not sent.”

04 / The tradeoff

Internal critique prioritized three concrete ambiguities before adding more features. The next study should test comprehension with fresh reviewers before expanding the listing set.

05 / Interaction and engineering

Preserve meaning through the journey.

The interaction work focused on preserving a candidate’s choices: returning focus after a view closes, keeping private notes during view updates and preventing an old Undo action from overwriting newer preferences. Save feedback also needs to distinguish a successful save from a failed write.

The resulting prototype uses explicit controls and shared evidence rules across discovery, details and comparison. The portfolio copy opens in memory-only preview mode, so its examples reset on reload and cannot alter the original ALIGN workspace.

Documented checks and their limits

Regression checks and enlarged-text layouts informed focus recovery, fixed-bar spacing, and the handling of private drafts. The next evaluation should combine real-device use with screen-reader and comprehension testing.

06 / Outcome and reflection

A coherent prototype. Questions still to test.

Created

A responsive candidate prototype, employer design exploration, evidence states and preserved visual alternatives.

Demonstrated

Connected exploration, comparison and explicit local confirmation, with implementation checks within their documented scope.

Unresolved

Usefulness beyond three fictional listings, independent comprehension, assistive-technology behavior and real delivery infrastructure.

The central lesson is that information helps only when its source and consequences stay clear. The next evaluation should ask fresh reviewers to distinguish unknown evidence from an explicit gap and predict what confirmation will do.

Project context: Independent, AI-assisted product concept. Listings and candidate data are fictional; the demo does not send applications. Evaluation to date consists of internal review.
